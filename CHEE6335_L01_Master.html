3Why Statistical Thermodynamics?

The technical part begins with a prediction question: why do liquids boil, rubber bands stretch and retract, and proteins catalyze reactions under particular conditions?

3.1 Measurable variables versus predictive thermodynamic variables

Pressure, temperature, volume, mass, and density are measurable. The lecture argues that these observations alone do not explain why a system suddenly changes state.

Class example: water close to its boiling point can undergo a large change from liquid to vapor after a small temperature increase. This motivates quantities such as entropy, free energy, and internal energy.

3.2 The molecular viewpoint

The instructor's central learning outcome is to think about what molecules are doing: where they can be, how they move, what energies they have, and what molecular arrangements are possible.

IMPORTANT
For a system containing an enormous number of molecules, many microscopic arrangements may be compatible with the same macroscopic constraints. Probability becomes essential.

3.3 Why probability enters thermodynamics

The lecture uses the air in the room as the key intuition. Oxygen and nitrogen remain mixed. Energy alone does not forbid extremely unusual arrangements, such as most oxygen collecting in one region, but such arrangements are overwhelmingly improbable.

REMEMBER
The conceptual bridge of Lecture 01 is: many molecules → many possible arrangements → probability → entropy later in the course.

4Foundations of Probability

Probability is introduced by counting how often an outcome appears among all trials.

4.1 Definition

Let N be the total number of trials and nA the number of trials with outcome A.

P(A)=\frac{n_A}{N}

In the instructor's intended numerical example:

P(A)=\frac{33}{330}=\frac{1}{10}=0.10

4.2 Counts must exhaust all trials

n_A+n_B+n_C+\cdots=N

4.3 Probabilities must exhaust all possibilities

P(A)+P(B)+P(C)+\cdots=1
EXAM
Always check that your complete set of outcome probabilities adds to 1.

توضیح فارسی

تعریف پایه‌ی احتمال بسیار ساده است: تعداد دفعاتی که نتیجه‌ی موردنظر رخ داده است را بر تعداد کل آزمایش‌ها تقسیم می‌کنیم.

اگر همه‌ی حالت‌های ممکن را فهرست کرده باشیم، مجموع تعداد آن‌ها باید برابر تعداد کل آزمایش‌ها باشد. به همین شکل، مجموع احتمال تمام حالت‌های ممکن باید برابر یک شود.

5Relationships Among Events

The lecture discusses several relationships among events. They describe different ideas and should not be treated as the same condition.

5.1 Mutually exclusive

Two outcomes are mutually exclusive when they cannot occur in the same trial. On one roll of a die, the result cannot be both 1 and 4.

5.2 Collectively exhaustive

A set of outcomes is collectively exhaustive when it includes every possible result.

P(A)+P(B)+P(C)+\cdots=1

5.3 Independent

Events A and B are independent when knowing that A happened does not change the probability of B.

P(B\mid A)=P(B)
COMMON MISTAKE
Mutually exclusive, independent, and collectively exhaustive are different relationships. The textbook presents them as separate definitions; do not combine them into one requirement.

توضیح فارسی

سه مفهوم این بخش را از هم جدا نگه دار.

حالت‌های ناسازگار نمی‌توانند در یک آزمایش هم‌زمان رخ دهند. حالت‌های جامع تمام امکان‌ها را پوشش می‌دهند. رویدادهای مستقل نیز روی احتمال یکدیگر اثر نمی‌گذارند.

6OR, AND & NOT

The instructor organizes the first probability rules using the words OR, AND, and NOT.

6.1 NOT

In this study edition, A′ means “not A.”

P(A')=1-P(A)

6.2 OR for mutually exclusive outcomes

If A and B cannot occur together, add their probabilities.

P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)

For one fair die:

P(1 or 4) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3

6.3 AND for independent events

If A and B are independent and both must occur, multiply.

P(AB)=P(A)P(B)

For a fair coin:

P(H)=P(T)=\frac12
P(H\text{ or }T)=1
REMEMBER
A quick reading rule: OR usually points toward addition; AND usually points toward multiplication - but first check overlap and independence.

برای متمم، در این جزوه از پرایم استفاده می‌کنیم. یعنی احتمال آ پرایم برابر یک منهای احتمال آ است.

اگر دو نتیجه نتوانند هم‌زمان رخ دهند و سؤال «یا» باشد، احتمال‌ها با هم جمع می‌شوند.

7Addition Rule & Overlap Correction

When A and B can both occur, simply adding P(A) and P(B) counts the overlap twice.

7.1 Complement route used in class

For independent A and B, the probability that neither happens is:

P(A'\text{ and }B')=[1-P(A)][1-P(B)]

Therefore, the probability that at least one happens is:

P(A\text{ or }B)=1-P(A'\text{ and }B')

Expanding gives:

P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A)P(B)

7.2 General addition rule

Without assuming independence, subtract the joint overlap once:

P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(AB)
EXAM
For “A or B or both,” think: add A + add B - subtract the overlap once.
COMMON MISTAKE
Do not use P(A)+P(B) when A and B can overlap. That double-counts the cases where both occur.

توضیح فارسی

اگر دو رویداد امکان هم‌زمان رخ دادن داشته باشند، جمع ساده‌ی دو احتمال اشتباه است؛ چون بخش مشترک را دو بار می‌شماری.

روش استاد ابتدا از حالت «هیچ‌کدام رخ ندهد» استفاده می‌کند و سپس متمم آن را می‌گیرد. نتیجه برای رویدادهای مستقل شامل یک جمله‌ی منفی برای حذف هم‌پوشانی است.

فرمول عمومی نیز همین منطق را دارد: احتمال آ و احتمال ب را جمع می‌کنیم و احتمال رخ دادن هم‌زمان آن‌ها را یک بار کم می‌کنیم.

8Composite Events: Two-Die Example

A composite event contains more than one trial. The two-die example is the main counting example of Lecture 01.

8.1 A specific sequence

First roll = 1 and second roll = 4. The rolls are independent:

P(1\text{ on first and }4\text{ on second})=\frac16\cdot\frac16=\frac1{36}

8.2 First roll = 1 OR second roll = 4, including both

There are 36 ordered outcomes in two rolls. Six have 1 on the first roll, six have 4 on the second roll, and one outcome belongs to both groups.

P(1\text{ on first or }4\text{ on second})=\frac{6+6-1}{36}=\frac{11}{36}
First = 16 outcomes
Second = 46 outcomes
Overlap1 outcome
Total favorable11 outcomes
TRANSCRIPT CORRECTION
Near the end of this example the transcript contains a stray “5/36.” That is inconsistent with the instructor’s own counting. The supported result is 11/36.

9Conditional & Joint Probability

Conditional probability is introduced when the result of one trial changes what can happen next.

9.1 Conditional probability

P(B\mid A)

Read this as: “the probability of B given A.”

9.2 Independence test

P(B\mid A)=P(B)

If this equality holds, A does not change the probability of B.

9.3 Joint probability / general multiplication rule

P(AB)=P(B\mid A)P(A)=P(A\mid B)P(B)

The textbook labels this the General Multiplication Rule (Bayes' Rule). The instructor also refers to it as the Bayes equation.

9.4 A priori and a posteriori

P(B)A priori probability: no condition is specified.
P(B|A)A posteriori / conditional probability: A is known to have occurred.
EXAM
Memorize the structure, not just the symbols: joint probability = probability of the first condition × conditional probability of the next event.

10Conditional Probability: Ball Example

The class uses two dark balls and one bright ball to show dependence caused by sampling without replacement.

10.1 Before any draw

P(\text{Bright})=\frac13,\qquad P(\text{Dark})=\frac23

10.2 Second draw if the first ball is not returned

P(\text{Bright}\mid\text{Bright first})=0
P(\text{Dark}\mid\text{Bright first})=1
P(\text{Bright}\mid\text{Dark first})=\frac12
P(\text{Dark}\mid\text{Dark first})=\frac12
First drawSecond drawJoint probability
BrightDark1/3 × 1 = 1/3
BrightBright1/3 × 0 = 0
DarkBright2/3 × 1/2 = 1/3
DarkDark2/3 × 1/2 = 1/3

10.3 Exhaustiveness check

\frac13+0+\frac13+\frac13=1
IMPORTANT
Without replacement, the second-draw probabilities depend on what happened on the first draw. With replacement, the original probabilities would be restored.

توضیح فارسی

در مثال کلاس سه توپ داریم: دو توپ تیره و یک توپ روشن. اگر توپ اول را برنگردانیم، ترکیب ظرف عوض می‌شود و در نتیجه احتمال مرحله‌ی دوم به نتیجه‌ی مرحله‌ی اول وابسته می‌شود.

اگر توپ روشن اول خارج شود، دیگر توپ روشن باقی نمی‌ماند. اگر توپ تیره اول خارج شود، یک توپ تیره و یک توپ روشن باقی می‌ماند و احتمال هرکدام در مرحله‌ی دوم برابر یک‌دوم می‌شود.

11Introductory Multiplicity

Multiplicity is introduced only at an intuitive level in Lecture 01. The formal combinatorics treatment comes in the next lecture.

11.1 Meaning

W denotes the number of possible ways or configurations associated with a situation.

11.2 Car-lot example

Suppose cars have two body styles and three colors. A body-style choice can be combined with each color choice.

W=(2\text{ body styles})(3\text{ colors})=6
REMEMBER
At this point, use multiplicity as a count of possible combinations. Do not jump ahead to the factorial formula; that is developed in Lecture 02.
